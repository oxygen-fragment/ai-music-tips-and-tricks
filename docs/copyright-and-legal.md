## Overview

This guide synthesizes copyright principles as they apply to song lyrics, focusing on when something breaches copyright versus when it's safe to use. It covers both U.S. and international (primarily EU) frameworks.

**Key Question:** Do lyrics need to be verbatim to breach copyright? Is there a similarity threshold?

**Short Answer:** No verbatim copying required for infringement. There's no fixed word count or percentage threshold. Courts apply a **qualitative test** based on whether the "protected expression" has been substantially copied, even if it's a short phrase that's distinctive.

---

## What Copyright Protects (Lyrics)

Copyright **protects the expression**, not the idea.

### Protected Elements

- **Specific wording of lyrics** - The exact words chosen and their arrangement
- **Structure and arrangement** - How verses, choruses, and phrases are organized
- **Creative expression** - Metaphors, imagery, and original word combinations
- **Distinctive phrases** - Even short phrases can be protected if they're sufficiently creative

### NOT Protected

- **Ideas, themes, moods, concepts** - "A song about heartbreak" is just an idea
- **Rhythms, genres, emotions** - "Upbeat pop song" or "melancholic ballad"
- **Common/stock phrases** - "I miss you", "baby come back", "I love you"
- **Thematic elements** - "Breakup song", "summer anthem", "party track"
- **Facts and titles** - Song titles alone generally aren't protected

### Legal Foundation

- **U.S.:** 17 U.S.C. §102(a) - Copyright protects "original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium"
- **EU:** "Author's own intellectual creation" standard (CJEU Infopaq case)
- **International:** WIPO/Berne Convention - Protection requires no formalities

---

## Idea-Expression Dichotomy

This is a fundamental copyright principle: **ideas are free; only their expression is protected.**

### Why Ideas Aren't Protected

1. **Society needs shared ideas** - Culture builds on common concepts
2. **Ideas are too vague** - They can't be precisely defined or verified
3. **Ideas aren't fixed** - Copyright requires fixation in tangible form
4. **Monopolies would stifle creativity** - Protecting ideas would freeze all future works

### Practical Example

- **Unprotected idea:** "A song about missing someone"
- **Protected expression:** "I miss you like the desert misses the rain" (specific metaphor and wording)

**Analogy:** Copyright protects *the sculpted statue, not the clay itself*.

---

## When Protection Begins

- Protection starts **immediately** when the work is fixed in tangible form
  - Writing lyrics in a notebook
  - Saving to a digital file
  - Recording a performance
- **No registration required** internationally (Berne Convention Art. 5(2))
- **No © symbol required** - Though it can serve as notice
- Author owns copyright automatically upon creation

---

## Registration (U.S. Only)

In the United States, registration with the U.S. Copyright Office is optional but provides significant strategic benefits.

### Benefits of Registration

- **Right to sue in federal court** - Required before filing infringement lawsuit
- **Statutory damages** - Can claim up to $150,000 per work (without proving actual damages)
- **Attorney's fees** - Court may award legal fees to prevailing party
- **Public record** - Creates official documentation of ownership
- **Presumption of validity** - If registered within 5 years of publication

### Important Notes

- Registration is **not required for ownership** - You own copyright the moment you create
- Registration only affects **enforcement options** in the U.S.
- Other countries don't require registration at all

## Rights the Author Holds

Both U.S. and EU copyright law grant these exclusive rights:

### Economic Rights

- **Reproduction** - Making copies of the work
- **Distribution** - Selling, renting, or giving away copies
- **Public performance** - Performing the work live or via recording in public
- **Public communication** - Streaming, broadcasting, making available online
- **Adaptation** - Creating derivative works (translations, remixes, arrangements)

### Moral Rights

Moral rights protect the author's personal connection to the work.

**Strong in EU:**
- **Attribution** - Right to be identified as the author
- **Integrity** - Right to object to distortion or mutilation of the work
- Cannot be waived in many EU countries

**Weak/Limited in U.S.:**
- Only applies to visual arts (paintings, sculptures)
- Generally does not apply to musical works
- Can be waived by contract

### Duration

**Standard term:** Life of the author + 70 years (Berne Convention standard)
- Applies in U.S., EU, and most major jurisdictions
- After expiration, work enters public domain

## Substantial Similarity

**Courts use qualitative, not quantitative, tests.**

There is **no magic number** of words that triggers infringement. Even copying a few words can be infringement if they're distinctive enough.

### United States Test

Two-part analysis:

1. **Copying-in-fact** - Did defendant actually copy (not create independently)?
2. **Unlawful appropriation** - Was protected expression taken?

**"Ordinary observer" test:** Would a typical person recognize the original work in the accused work?

### European Union Test

- Does the accused work reproduce the author's **"intellectual creation"**?
- Focus on whether the distinctive creative choices were copied
- No numeric thresholds

### Key Principles

- **Quality over quantity** - A short but distinctive phrase can infringe
- **Context matters** - Common phrases in new combinations may be protected
- **De minimis exception** - Truly trivial copying may not be actionable
- **No safe harbor** - There's no "X words are safe" rule

### Example

**Short but distinctive phrase:** "hit me baby one more time"
- Only 6 words
- But highly distinctive and associated with specific work
- Could be infringement depending on context of use

---

## Derivative Works

A derivative work is one based on or derived from an existing work.

### What Requires Permission

- **Remixes** - Using the original recording or substantial lyrics
- **Translations** - Converting lyrics to another language while keeping structure
- **Adaptations** - Changing the work while keeping identifiable elements
- **Mashups** - Combining recognizable portions of protected works

### What Doesn't Require Permission

- **Transforming the idea** - Creating something inspired by the concept but with new expression
- **Independent creation** - Writing similar lyrics without copying (though hard to prove)
- **Using unprotected elements** - Building on ideas, themes, common phrases

### Example

**Requires permission:**
- Translating "Imagine there's no heaven..." to Spanish while keeping the structure

**Doesn't require permission:**
- Writing a completely new song about imagining a peaceful world (same idea, new expression)

## Fair Use (U.S.) / Exceptions (EU)

### United States: Fair Use

Fair use is a defense to copyright infringement based on four statutory factors (17 U.S.C. §107):

#### The Four Factors

1. **Purpose and character of use**
   - Is it transformative?
   - Commercial vs. educational/commentary
   - Does it add new meaning or message?

2. **Nature of the copyrighted work**
   - Highly creative works (like lyrics) get more protection
   - Published vs. unpublished

3. **Amount and substantiality**
   - How much was taken?
   - Was the "heart" of the work used?

4. **Effect on the market**
   - Does it serve as a substitute for the original?
   - Does it harm the market for the original or derivatives?
   - Market harm weighs heavily against fair use

#### Fair Use and Lyrics

- **Lyrics are highly protected** - They're purely creative expression
- **Short excerpts often fail fair use** - Even brief quotes can fail if they substitute for the original
- **Commentary/criticism has stronger case** - Using lyrics to comment *on the song itself*
- **Educational use is not automatic** - Still must pass all four factors

### European Union: Specific Exceptions

The EU has **no broad fair use doctrine**. Instead, there's a closed list of specific exceptions that vary by member state.

#### Common Exceptions

- **Quotation exception** - Must be:
  - Proportionate to the purpose
  - Contextual (for criticism, review, or commentary)
  - Properly attributed

- **Parody exception** - Post-Deckmyn v. Vandersteen (2014)
  - Must evoke existing work while being noticeably different
  - Must constitute expression of humor or mockery

- **Research and private study** - Very limited, typically not commercial

- **Educational purposes** - Limited and varies by country

#### Key Differences from U.S.

- **No flexible balancing test** - Must fit within specific exception
- **Member state variation** - Each EU country implements differently
- **Stricter limitations** - Generally less flexible than U.S. fair use

## Parody vs Satire

This distinction is **critical** in copyright law and often determines whether use is allowed.

### Parody

**Definition:** Uses the original work to comment **on or criticize the original itself**.

**Characteristics:**
- Targets the original work, its style, or its message
- Makes fun of or critiques the original
- Conjures up the original to mock it
- Must copy enough to identify the original being parodied

**Legal Protection:**
- **Stronger protection** in U.S. fair use analysis
- **Recognized exception** in EU law (post-Deckmyn)
- Only copy what's necessary to "conjure up" the original

**Example:**
"Weird Al" Yankovic's work often qualifies as parody (though he gets permission anyway)

### Satire

**Definition:** Uses the original work to comment **on something else** (society, politics, current events).

**Characteristics:**
- Uses the work as a vehicle to mock something unrelated
- The original work is just a convenient tool
- Doesn't comment on the original work itself

**Legal Protection:**
- **Weak or no protection** in most cases
- Usually constitutes infringement without license
- Cannot justify copying someone else's expression to mock something else

**Example:**
Using a popular song's melody/lyrics to mock a political figure (not the song)

### Why the Distinction Matters

**Parody:** "I need to copy this work because *this work itself* is what I'm commenting on"
✓ More likely to be allowed

**Satire:** "I'm borrowing this work because it's popular/convenient for my unrelated message"
✗ Usually infringement

## Examples: Successful Parodies

### Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music (1994) - 2 Live Crew

**Original:** Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman"
**Parody:** 2 Live Crew's rap version

**Why it won:**
- Directly commented on the original's romantic view of women
- Transformed the message (from earnest to satirical)
- Copied only what was necessary to identify original
- Criticized and mocked the original song's tone and sentiment
- Highly transformative

**Significance:** Landmark Supreme Court case establishing that commercial parody can be fair use

### Elsmere Music v. NBC (1980s)

**Original:** "I Love New York" tourism jingle
**Parody:** Saturday Night Live's "I Love Sodom"

**Why it won:**
- Direct critique of the original phrase and its cultural messaging
- Mocked the specific campaign, not just New York generally
- Brief, transformative use
- Clear parody intent

## Examples: Failed Parodies

### Dr. Seuss Enterprises v. Penguin Books (1997)

**Original:** Dr. Seuss books (distinctive style and art)
**Accused work:** "The Cat NOT in the Hat" about O.J. Simpson trial

**Why it failed:**
- Mocked the O.J. trial, **not Dr. Seuss's work**
- Used Seuss style merely as a vehicle for unrelated satire
- No commentary on Dr. Seuss's original message or style
- Classic example of satire, not parody

### Air Pirates v. Disney (1978)

**Original:** Disney's Mickey Mouse and other characters
**Accused work:** Adult underground comics featuring Disney characters

**Why it failed:**
- Mocked American culture and values, not Disney's work specifically
- Excessive copying beyond what was necessary
- No legitimate commentary on Disney's creative expression
- Used Disney characters as vehicle for unrelated social commentary

### "On Top of Spaghetti" (Abilene Music)

**Original:** "On Top of Old Smokey"
**Accused work:** Novelty song "On Top of Spaghetti"

**Why it failed (in some circuits):**
- Humorous but didn't criticize the original song
- Simple substitution for comedic effect
- No commentary on the original work's message or style
- More of a novelty cover than transformative parody

## Using Lyrics Legally

### Example: "hit me baby, one more time"

This iconic phrase illustrates various legal use scenarios.

#### A) Parody (May Be Allowed)

Transform it to comment directly on the original:

**Example:** "Sue me, Britney, one more time"

**Requirements:**
- Must mock the original song, Britney's persona, or pop music phenomenon
- Cannot serve as market substitute
- Must be transformative
- Should use only what's necessary to evoke the original

#### B) Quotation (Limited)

**U.S.:** Only if sufficiently transformative
- Using in a review or criticism of the song
- Academic analysis with commentary
- Documentary about pop music history

**EU:** Under quotation exception
- Must be attributed
- Proportionate to purpose
- Contextual (review, criticism, commentary)
- Cannot substitute for the original

#### C) Incidental Capture

Accidental background capture in video or audio recording.

**Note:** This varies significantly by jurisdiction and is extremely limited. Not reliable.

#### D) Licensing (Always Safe)

Obtain proper license:

- **Mechanical license** - For recording/distributing cover version
- **Sync license** - For use in video/film
- **Sample clearance** - For using in new recording
- **Lyric reprint license** - For printing lyrics

Contact:
- Publisher or rights holder
- Performing rights organizations (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC in U.S.)
- Licensing services (Harry Fox Agency, etc.)

#### E) Transform the Idea (Generally Safe)

Rewrite entirely with new expression:

**Original idea:** "Ask me to hurt you repeatedly"
**New expression:** "Strike me twice, darling"

**Why it's different:**
- Different words, different structure
- Same general idea but new creative expression
- Ideas aren't protected, only expression

## Monitoring by Publishers and Platforms

Publishers and platforms use sophisticated automated systems to detect copyright infringement.

### A) Text-Matching Systems

**Technologies used:**
- **Tokenization** - Breaking text into words/phrases
- **N-gram analysis** - Comparing sequences of N consecutive words
- **Fuzzy matching** - Detecting similar but not identical text
- **Similarity thresholds** - Commonly 70-90% similarity triggers review

**What triggers detection:**

- **5-10 consecutive matching words** - Common threshold for investigation
- **Highly unique phrases** - Even 3-5 words if distinctive
  - "hit me baby one more time" (6 words, but extremely distinctive)
- **Repeated hooks or chorus fragments** - Multiple instances of similar phrases
- **Title + opening line matches** - Strong indicator of copying

**Used by:**
- Lyric websites (Genius, AZLyrics) - To detect unauthorized postings
- Publishers - To find unauthorized reproductions
- Content platforms - To screen user-generated content

### B) Audio Fingerprinting

**Technologies:**
- **Acoustic fingerprinting** - Converting audio to unique digital signatures
- **Spectral analysis** - Breaking down frequency components
- **Database comparison** - Matching against millions of licensed tracks
- **Real-time detection** - Identifying music as it's streamed/uploaded

**Platforms using this technology:**
- **YouTube** - Content ID system
- **Twitch** - Soundtrack by Twitch + Audible Magic
- **TikTok** - Commercial music detection
- **Meta (Facebook/Instagram)** - Rights Manager
- **Spotify** - Internal monitoring systems

**Twitch specifics:**
- Live detection is limited (processing intensive)
- Primarily mutes or issues strikes on **VODs (Video on Demand)**
- Uses Audible Magic-style scanning
- DMCA system relies heavily on publisher takedown notices
- Past controversies over mass-muting VODs

### C) Publisher Scrapers

Major music publishers run automated web crawlers:

**What they do:**
- Scan lyric websites, blogs, forums
- Monitor video platforms
- Identify unauthorized reproductions
- Auto-generate DMCA takedown notices

**How they work:**
- Crawl public web pages
- Compare found text against database of protected works
- Flag matches above similarity threshold
- Send automated or semi-automated takedown requests

**Major players:**
- Universal Music Publishing
- Sony Music Publishing
- Warner Chappell Music
- BMG Rights Management
- Kobalt Music Group

## How Copyright Transfers

Copyright is property that can be transferred or licensed.

### A) Assignment

**Definition:** Written agreement transferring **complete ownership** of copyright.

**Characteristics:**
- Seller no longer owns the copyright
- Buyer becomes the full copyright owner
- Equivalent to selling real estate
- Must be in writing to be valid (U.S. and most jurisdictions)
- Can be for all rights or specific rights (e.g., only film rights)

**Example:**
"I hereby assign all copyright in my song 'Example Song' to Publisher Co."

### B) Exclusive License

**Definition:** Author retains ownership but grants exclusive rights to licensee.

**Characteristics:**
- Author keeps ownership/title
- Licensee has exclusive permission to use the work
- No one else (including the author) can exercise those rights
- Licensee may be able to enforce rights against infringers
- Must be in writing (in most jurisdictions)
- Can be limited by time, territory, or medium

**Example:**
"I grant Publisher Co. the exclusive right to print and distribute my lyrics in North America for 10 years."

### C) Work-for-Hire

**Definition:** The employer/commissioner is considered the legal author from creation.

**U.S. law (17 U.S.C. §101, 201):**

Two scenarios:
1. **Employee creation** - Work created by employee within scope of employment
2. **Commissioned work** - Only for specific categories if written agreement exists:
   - Contribution to collective work
   - Part of audiovisual work
   - Translation
   - Supplementary work
   - Compilation
   - Instructional text
   - Test or answer material
   - Atlas

**EU/International:**
- Concept not broadly recognized
- Employer obtains economic rights by default in many countries
- Author retains moral rights (inalienable in many EU states)
- Specific contracts needed to clarify ownership

**Why it matters:**
- Author never owns the copyright (employer owns from creation)
- No need for assignment
- Important for songwriters working for labels or studios

## Core Rules for Legally Safe Parody

If you want to create parody that's defensible, follow these guidelines:

### 1. Target the Original

- Your parody **must comment on the original work**
- Mock the original's style, message, tone, or cultural phenomenon
- Not just: "I'm using this because it's popular"

### 2. Copy Only What's Necessary

- Use only enough to "conjure up" the original in the audience's mind
- Don't copy more than needed to make the parody obvious
- Transform everything else

### 3. Transform Meaning, Tone, and Purpose

- Change the message significantly
- Flip the tone (serious → funny, romantic → cynical)
- Add new expression and meaning
- Don't just slightly modify the original

### 4. Cannot Serve as Market Substitute

- Your parody must not replace the original in the market
- People shouldn't buy/use your parody instead of the original
- It must serve a different purpose

### 5. The Joke Fails Without the Original

- The parody should only make sense in reference to the original
- If someone unfamiliar with the original can't understand the joke, it's probably parody
- If it works independently, it might be satire (weaker protection)

### 6. Make It Obviously Parody

- The parody should be clear and noticeable
- Don't try to disguise it as the original
- Audience should immediately recognize it's a parody

### 7. Label It as Parody (Practical, Not Legal)

**Note:** Labeling doesn't make something legally a parody, but it helps:
- Demonstrate your intent
- Reduce confusion
- Show good faith

## Practical Guidance for Creators & Developers

### For AI Music Creators

**To reduce copyright risk:**

1. **Avoid verbatim copying**
   - Don't use famous lyrics word-for-word
   - Even a few distinctive words can trigger issues

2. **Generate unique expression**
   - Create original metaphors and imagery
   - Use your own sentence structure
   - Develop unique phrasings

3. **Transform ideas, not expression**
   - Borrow concepts and themes freely
   - Express them in completely new words

4. **For parody, be strict**
   - Follow all Campbell criteria
   - Make sure it targets the original work
   - Don't use more than necessary

5. **Check AI outputs**
   - AI models may reproduce protected lyrics
   - Review outputs for substantial similarity to existing works
   - Regenerate if output is too similar to known songs

6. **When in doubt, get permission**
   - Licensing is the safest route
   - Publishers are often open to reasonable uses

### For Educational/Commentary Use

**Under U.S. fair use or EU quotation exception:**

1. **Add significant commentary**
   - Don't just reprint lyrics
   - Provide analysis, criticism, or context

2. **Use proportionally**
   - Quote only what's necessary for your point
   - Don't reproduce entire songs

3. **Attribute properly**
   - Credit the songwriter and performer
   - Provide source information

4. **Transformative purpose**
   - Your use should add new meaning or message
   - Not serve as substitute for original

### For Platform Developers

**Content filtering considerations:**

1. **Implement text-matching systems**
   - Detect 5+ consecutive matching words
   - Flag distinctive phrases
   - Use fuzzy matching for variations

2. **Audio fingerprinting**
   - Integrate with services like Audible Magic
   - Match against licensed music databases
   - Real-time detection where feasible

3. **DMCA compliance (U.S.)**
   - Implement proper takedown procedures
   - Respond promptly to notices
   - Maintain safe harbor protections

4. **User education**
   - Warn users about copyright issues
   - Provide guidance on legal use
   - Explain parody requirements

## Key Takeaways

### Core Principles

1. **Lyrics = highly protected creative expression**
   - Among the most protected forms of copyright
   - Even short distinctive phrases can be protected

2. **Ideas and themes = free to use**
   - No one owns "a song about heartbreak"
   - Concepts, moods, and genres are unprotected

3. **No magic number threshold**
   - Courts use qualitative tests, not word counts
   - 5 distinctive words can infringe; 50 generic words might not

4. **Expression is what's protected globally**
   - U.S., EU, and Berne Convention countries all protect original expression
   - Duration: Life + 70 years in most jurisdictions

### Defenses and Exceptions

5. **Parody = powerful but narrow defense**
   - Must target and comment on the original work
   - Copy only what's necessary
   - Transformative purpose required
   - Stronger in U.S.; recognized but limited in EU

6. **Satire = almost always infringement**
   - Using a work to mock something else
   - Usually requires license
   - Not protected under fair use or EU exceptions

7. **Fair use (U.S.) is case-by-case**
   - Four-factor test with no guaranteed outcomes
   - Transformative, non-substituting uses have stronger case
   - Market harm weighs heavily

8. **EU exceptions are specific and limited**
   - No broad fair use doctrine
   - Must fit within closed list of exceptions
   - Quotation and parody are most relevant for lyrics

### Detection and Enforcement

9. **Automated detection is aggressive**
   - Text-matching, audio fingerprinting widely deployed
   - Often over-inclusive (flags lawful uses)
   - Publishers actively monitor and send takedowns

10. **Platforms use sophisticated scanning**
    - YouTube Content ID, Twitch Audible Magic, etc.
    - Real-time and VOD monitoring
    - DMCA system for user-generated content

### Rights Management

11. **Registration (U.S.) is optional but powerful**
    - Not required for ownership
    - Enables statutory damages and attorney's fees
    - Required before filing lawsuit

12. **Copyright transfer requires written agreements**
    - Assignment = full ownership transfer
    - Exclusive license = author retains ownership
    - Work-for-hire = employer is author from creation

### Practical Advice

13. **For AI-generated content:**
    - Ensure outputs are not substantially similar to existing lyrics
    - Generate unique metaphors and phrasings
    - Review before publishing

14. **When using existing lyrics:**
    - Get permission/license (safest)
    - Use parody defense carefully (must target original)
    - Avoid market substitution
    - Attribute and use proportionally for commentary

15. **If uncertain, seek legal advice**
    - Copyright law is complex and fact-specific
    - Professional counsel is essential for high-risk situations
    - Licensing is often more cost-effective than litigation
